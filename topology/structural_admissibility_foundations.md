## Purpose

Topological Integrity Gravity (TIG) begins from a simple observation:

Not every mathematically valid geometry must correspond to a physically admissible reality.

Modern gravitational theories typically focus on constructing equations whose solutions satisfy mathematical consistency. However, mathematical existence alone does not guarantee physical admissibility.

The central question of TIG is therefore:

> Which structural conditions must be satisfied for a geometric configuration to remain physically admissible?

This shifts the focus from arbitrary solution generation toward admissibility itself.

---

# The Admissibility Principle

The core assumption of TIG is:

> Physical reality is constrained not only by mathematical consistency, but also by structural admissibility.

A geometry may be mathematically constructible while still violating fundamental requirements necessary for persistent physical existence.

TIG therefore introduces admissibility as a primary selection principle.

---

# Mathematical Possibility vs Physical Admissibility

A distinction must be made between mathematical possibility and physical admissibility.

Physical admissibility represents a stronger condition than mathematical existence.

---

# Structural Integrity

TIG proposes that admissible configurations must preserve structural integrity.

Structural integrity refers to the ability of a configuration to maintain coherent state relationships under evolution.

---

# Persistence

A fundamental requirement for physical reality is persistence.

Persistence therefore becomes a structural requirement rather than an emergent convenience.

---

# Horizon Formation as an Admissibility Problem

Within TIG1, horizon formation is not viewed merely as a geometric consequence.

Instead, horizon formation becomes a structural admissibility problem.

# Operational Definition of Admissibility

For the purposes of the Topological Foundations Program, a structure is considered admissible if it simultaneously satisfies:

- persistence,
- integrity,
- connectivity,
- addressability,
- recursive stability.

A structure failing any of these conditions is considered structurally non-admissible.

Formally:

A(S) = 1

if all admissibility criteria are satisfied.

A(S) = 0

otherwise.

This definition remains provisional and will be refined as quantitative measures become available.
