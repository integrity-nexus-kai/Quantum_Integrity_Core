\begin{abstract}
We analyze the horizon structure of a specified static, spherically symmetric
representative geometry within the Topological Integrity Gravity (TIG) research
program. The chosen regular mass profile yields the exact dimensionless horizon
equation \(x^3-x^2+\beta^3=0\). We derive its discriminant, positive-root sectors,
and critical point \(x_c=2/3\), \(\beta_c=(4/27)^{1/3}\). The two positive roots
coalesce in a nondegenerate local fold with square-root separation. The cubic
is a consequence of the chosen mass profile, not a universal requirement of
fold bifurcations or dimensional consistency. A direct trace test also excludes
this nontrivial representative metric as an exact vacuum solution of
constant-coefficient quadratic metric \(f(R)\) gravity. The Schwarzschild limit
and the bounded-curvature core remain geometric properties of the specified
model; dynamical completion and observational forecasts remain separate tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

General Relativity provides the most successful classical description of
gravitation. Its geometric formulation identifies gravitational interaction with
the curvature of spacetime and has passed a wide range of observational tests
since Einstein's formulation of the gravitational field equations
\cite{einstein1915,wald1984,carroll2004}. Nevertheless, the appearance of
singularities and the structure of horizon formation indicate that the classical
theory may not fully encode all structural constraints relevant at extreme
curvature scales.

A common strategy is to modify the gravitational action by adding curvature
corrections. Such models are often written as extensions of the Einstein-Hilbert
action through a generalized function \(f(R)\) of the Ricci scalar. These
approaches form a broad and well-studied class of modified gravitational
theories \cite{sotiriou2010,nojiri2011,faraoni2010}. However, this procedure may
introduce an overly large space of possible theories. If curvature corrections
are chosen freely, the resulting model space becomes phenomenological rather
than structurally constrained.

The purpose of this work is to formulate a minimal alternative viewpoint. We
assume that admissible curvature corrections are restricted by consistency
conditions. In this sense, the question is not merely which correction terms can
be written down, but which correction terms preserve the structural integrity of
horizon formation.

We call this framework \emph{Topological Integrity Gravity} (TIG). The term
``topological integrity'' is used here in a restricted mathematical sense: it
refers to the preservation of the qualitative root structure associated with
horizon formation under admissible curvature corrections.

The central result of this paper is the exact horizon equation of the chosen
representative mass profile,
\begin{equation}
x^3 - x^2 + \beta^3 = 0,
\label{eq:intro_cubic}
\end{equation}
where \(x\) is a dimensionless radial horizon variable and \(\beta\) is a
dimensionless structural control parameter. This equation exhibits a critical
transition: for sufficiently small \(\beta\), horizon solutions exist; beyond a
critical value, no positive horizon root remains.

The transition follows from the algebraic horizon equation once the mass
profile has been specified. Structural consistency requirements alone do not
select that profile uniquely. The resulting critical point is a boundary
between horizon and non-horizon configurations within this model. The associated root coalescence is structurally analogous to
fold-type criticality and saddle-node bifurcations known from catastrophe theory
\cite{arnold1992}.

\section{Geometric and Physical Setup}

We consider a four-dimensional spacetime \((M,g)\) equipped with a Lorentzian
metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\). The discussion is restricted to static, spherically
symmetric configurations. The general line element may be written in the form
\begin{equation}
ds^2 = -A(r)c^2dt^2 + B(r)dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2,
\label{eq:metric}
\end{equation}
where \(d\Omega^2\) denotes the metric on the unit sphere.

In the Schwarzschild solution of General Relativity, the horizon is located at
the radial coordinate
\begin{equation}
r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2},
\label{eq:schwarzschild_radius}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) is the mass of the central object \cite{schwarzschild1916}. The
Schwarzschild geometry remains the canonical reference solution for static black
holes in General Relativity \cite{wald1984,chandrasekhar1983}.

In the one-function static sector used below, candidate Killing horizons are
located at positive zeros of the lapse function,
\begin{equation}
A(r)=0.
\label{eq:horizon_condition}
\end{equation}

In modified or structurally constrained theories, the functional form of
\(A(r)\) may receive corrections. The key question is whether such corrections
preserve a well-defined horizon structure.

\begin{definition}[Dimensionless horizon variable]
We define the dimensionless radial variable
\begin{equation}
x := \frac{r}{r_s}.
\label{eq:x_definition}
\end{equation}
The Schwarzschild horizon corresponds to \(x=1\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Structural control parameter]
Let \(\ell\) denote a characteristic correction length scale associated with the
regularization or structural modification of the central region. We define the
dimensionless parameter
\begin{equation}
\beta := \frac{\ell}{r_s}.
\label{eq:beta_definition}
\end{equation}
The regime \(\beta \ll 1\) corresponds to corrections much smaller than the
Schwarzschild scale.
\end{definition}

Below we use geometrized units \(G=c=1\), with \(M\) denoting the mass in
length units, \(r_s=2M\), and \(\ell=r_c\). The analyzed sector has
\(A(r)=F(r)\) and \(B(r)=1/F(r)\); it does not exhaust general static spherical
metrics. Counting its lapse zeros does not establish a dynamical formation
process or the global event-horizon structure of an evolving spacetime.

The limit \(\beta=0\) must recover the classical Schwarzschild horizon. This
requirement imposes a normalization condition on any admissible horizon
equation: the dimensionless equation must admit \(x=1\) as the physical root
when \(\beta=0\).

\section{Structural Consistency Principle}

The guiding assumption of TIG is that gravitational corrections are not
arbitrary deformations. Instead, they must preserve the qualitative integrity of
the horizon formation problem.

\begin{definition}[Structural horizon consistency]
A horizon equation is structurally consistent if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item it admits the Schwarzschild limit \(x=1\) when the correction parameter
vanishes;
\item it has a well-defined algebraic root structure;
\item horizon disappearance, if it occurs, takes place through a critical root
coalescence rather than by arbitrary removal of solutions;
\item the correction terms are dimensionless and compatible with the chosen
normalization;
\item the resulting transition can be analyzed through invariant algebraic
quantities such as the discriminant.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

These conditions are minimal. They do not specify a complete gravitational field
theory. Instead, they constrain the admissible reduced horizon equations.

These requirements do not determine a polynomial degree or a unique mass
profile. A local fold has an equilibrium equation of the form
\begin{equation}
y^2-\lambda=0,
\end{equation}
which is quadratic; a corresponding potential can be cubic
\cite{arnold1992,chasnov2020}. For example,
\(Q(x,\lambda)=x^2-x+\lambda\) has the Schwarzschild-normalized root \(x=1\)
at \(\lambda=0\) and a fold at \((x,\lambda)=(1/2,1/4)\).
Adding a double zero-radius factor is an additional model choice, not a
consequence of fold criticality. Likewise, dimensional consistency of two
dimensionless variables does not require a \(\beta^3\) correction.

\section{Derivation of the Specific Cubic Horizon Equation}

Choose \(M>0\), \(r_c>0\), and the representative mass profile
\begin{equation}
m(r)=\frac{M r^3}{r^3+r_c^3},\qquad
F(r)=1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}.
\label{eq:chosen_mass_profile}
\end{equation}
It has \(m(r)\to M\) at infinity and \(m(r)=O(r^3)\) near the center.
These requirements do not make the interpolation unique: for example,
\(\widetilde m(r)=M r^3/(r^2+r_c^2)^{3/2}\) has the same two limiting
properties and gives a different horizon equation.

For the chosen profile and positive horizon radius, \(F(r_H)=0\) gives
\begin{equation}
r_H^3-2Mr_H^2+r_c^3=0.
\end{equation}
With \(x=r_H/(2M)\) and \(\beta=r_c/(2M)\), division by \((2M)^3\) yields
\begin{equation}
P(x,\beta)=x^3-x^2+\beta^3=0.
\label{eq:tig_horizon_equation}
\end{equation}
Indeed, \(F=P/(x^3+\beta^3)\); the denominator is positive for
\(x>0\), \(\beta>0\). The \(\beta^3\) term comes from the chosen
\(r_c^3\) in the mass-profile denominator, not from dimensional analysis
alone. This is an exact reduced horizon equation for the specified geometry.

At \(\beta=0\), clearing the denominator yields \(P_0=x^2(x-1)\).
The double zero at \(x=0\) is outside the equivalence domain and is not an
additional Schwarzschild horizon or a derived internal physical sector.
For \(r_c>0\), the regular center has \(F(0)=1\).

\begin{remark}
Equation \eqref{eq:tig_horizon_equation} is not a fundamental field equation
or a unique consequence of the consistency principle. Its cubic degree is a
property of the selected representative geometry.
\end{remark}

\section{Root Structure and Criticality}

The cubic equation
\begin{equation}
x^3-x^2+\beta^3=0
\end{equation}
has a critical point when two real roots coalesce. This occurs when the
polynomial and its derivative vanish simultaneously.

The derivative is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dP}{dx}=3x^2-2x=x(3x-2).
\label{eq:derivative}
\end{equation}

The nonzero stationary point is therefore
\begin{equation}
x_c=\frac{2}{3}.
\label{eq:critical_x}
\end{equation}

At criticality, we impose
\begin{equation}
P(x_c,\beta_c)=0.
\end{equation}
Substituting \(x_c=2/3\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3
-
\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2
+
\beta_c^3
=0.
\end{equation}
This gives
\begin{equation}
\beta_c^3=\frac{4}{27},
\label{eq:beta_c_cubed}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
\beta_c=\left(\frac{4}{27}\right)^{1/3}.
\label{eq:beta_c}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Critical horizon transition]
The TIG horizon equation admits a critical transition at
\begin{equation}
\beta_c=\left(\frac{4}{27}\right)^{1/3}.
\end{equation}
At this point, two real roots coalesce at
\begin{equation}
x_c=\frac{2}{3}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A double root occurs when \(P(x,\beta)=0\) and \(P'(x,\beta)=0\). Since
\(P'(x)=x(3x-2)\), the nonzero candidate is \(x=2/3\). Substitution into
\(P(x,\beta)=0\) yields \(\beta_c^3=4/27\), completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Discriminant Analysis}

The cubic equation
\begin{equation}
x^3-x^2+\beta^3=0
\end{equation}
can be written in the standard form
\begin{equation}
x^3+ax^2+bx+c=0,
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
a=-1,\qquad b=0,\qquad c=\beta^3.
\end{equation}

The discriminant of a cubic is
\begin{equation}
\Delta
=
a^2b^2
-4b^3
-4a^3c
-27c^2
+18abc.
\label{eq:cubic_discriminant}
\end{equation}
Substituting \(a=-1\), \(b=0\), and \(c=\beta^3\), we find
\begin{equation}
\Delta=4\beta^3-27\beta^6.
\label{eq:discriminant_beta}
\end{equation}
Factoring,
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\beta^3(4-27\beta^3).
\label{eq:discriminant_factored}
\end{equation}

The discriminant vanishes when \(\beta=0\) or when
\begin{equation}
\beta_c^3=\frac{4}{27}.
\end{equation}
The sign of the discriminant determines the real root structure:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(\Delta>0\): three distinct real roots;
\item \(\Delta=0\): multiple roots;
\item \(\Delta<0\): one real root and two complex conjugate roots.
\end{itemize}

The discriminant therefore provides an invariant algebraic diagnostic of the
structural transition.

\section{Branch Geometry and Physical Interpretation}

The positive roots of the horizon equation correspond to candidate horizon
locations. In the Schwarzschild limit \(\beta=0\), the equation becomes
\begin{equation}
x^3-x^2=x^2(x-1)=0.
\end{equation}
The physical nonzero root is
\begin{equation}
x=1.
\end{equation}

For \(0<\beta<\beta_c\), there are exactly two positive roots and one negative
root. The positive roots follow from \(P(0)>0\),
\(P(2/3)=\beta^3-4/27<0\), and the monotonicity on either side of the minimum.
The negative root is outside the radial domain. The outer positive root is
continuously deformed away from the Schwarzschild value. As \(\beta\) increases, the relevant positive roots
approach each other. At \(\beta=\beta_c\), the roots coalesce at \(x=2/3\).
For \(\beta>\beta_c\), the corresponding pair of real roots no longer exists.

At criticality, \(P=(x-2/3)^2(x+1/3)\); above criticality its only real root
is negative. Thus the count of positive horizon roots changes from two to zero
through one positive double root. The branch locations are continuous up to
coalescence; it is their number that changes. This is a model-specific static
horizon transition, not a proof of dynamical black-hole formation.

\section{Comparison with General Relativity}

For every fixed \(r>0\), the limit \(r_c\to0\) of the chosen geometry is
\(F(r)=1-2M/r\), and its outer horizon tends to \(r=2M\) or \(x=1\).
This is a metric-level Schwarzschild recovery, not a derivation of the full
Einstein dynamics from a completed TIG action. The limit is not uniform at the
center, where the Schwarzschild geometry is singular.

\section{Scaling Near Criticality}

Near the critical point, root coalescence implies non-linear sensitivity. Let
\begin{equation}
\epsilon = \beta_c-\beta.
\end{equation}
For small positive \(\epsilon\), the separation between the coalescing roots is
expected to scale as a fractional power of \(\epsilon\).

To see this, expand \(P(x,\beta)\) near the critical point:
\begin{equation}
x=x_c+\delta,
\qquad
\beta=\beta_c-\epsilon.
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
P(x_c,\beta_c)=0,
\qquad
P_x(x_c,\beta_c)=0,
\end{equation}
the leading expansion is
\begin{equation}
P(x,\beta)
\approx
\frac{1}{2}P_{xx}(x_c,\beta_c)\delta^2
+
P_{\beta}(x_c,\beta_c)(-\epsilon).
\end{equation}
The derivatives are
\begin{equation}
P_{xx}=6x-2,
\qquad
P_{\beta}=3\beta^2.
\end{equation}
At \(x_c=2/3\), one has \(P_{xx}(x_c)=2\). Hence
\begin{equation}
\delta^2\sim 3\beta_c^2\epsilon,
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\delta_\pm=\pm\sqrt{3}\,\beta_c\epsilon^{1/2}+O(\epsilon).
\label{eq:sqrt_scaling}
\end{equation}

Here \(P_{xx}(x_c,\beta_c)=2\ne0\) and
\(P_\beta(x_c,\beta_c)=3\beta_c^2\ne0\), establishing a nondegenerate local
fold \cite{arnold1992,chasnov2020}. The exponent refers to horizon-root
displacement in this control parameter; it does not by itself fix an echo,
shadow, or perturbation exponent or imply dynamical stability.

\section{Representative Effective Geometry}

For illustration, one may consider a representative bounded-curvature lapse
function of the form
\begin{equation}
F(r)=1-\frac{2M r^2}{r^3+r_c^3},
\label{eq:effective_lapse}
\end{equation}
where \(r_c\) is a regulated core scale. This expression is not introduced as a
complete solution of new covariant field equations. It is a representative
geometry used to examine the compatibility of the cubic horizon structure with a
regularized high-curvature sector.

For large radial distance, \(r\gg r_c\), one has
\begin{equation}
F(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{2M r_c^3}{r^4}+O(r^{-7}),
\end{equation}
so the Schwarzschild-compatible asymptotic behavior is recovered.

Near the origin, the lapse takes the form
\begin{equation}
F(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r_c^3}r^2+O(r^5),
\end{equation}
which corresponds to a regular de Sitter-like core in this representative
sector. This does not constitute a general singularity-resolution theorem; it
only states that the analyzed representative geometry has finite central
behavior.


\subsection{Test Against Quadratic Metric Gravity}

For comparison, consider the metric action
\(S_g=(16\pi)^{-1}\int\sqrt{-g}(R-2\Lambda+\mu R^2)\,d^4x\),
with constant finite \(\mu\) and \(\Lambda\). Its vacuum trace equation is
\begin{equation}
6\mu\Box R-R+4\Lambda=0
\label{eq:vacuum_trace_test}
\end{equation}
\cite{sotiriou2010,defelice2010}. For the representative geometry, writing
\(a=r_c^3\), direct curvature calculation gives
\begin{equation}
R(r)=\frac{12Ma(2a-r^3)}{(r^3+a)^3},\qquad
\Box R=\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2F\frac{dR}{dr}\right).
\end{equation}
At infinity the trace residual tends to \(4\Lambda\), so vacuum compatibility
requires \(\Lambda=0\). With that value,
\begin{equation}
\lim_{r\to\infty}r^6(6\mu\Box R-R)=12Ma\ne0,
\qquad
\lim_{r\to0^+}(6\mu\Box R-R)=-\frac{24M}{a}\ne0.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for \(M>0\) and \(r_c>0\), this metric is not an exact vacuum
solution of constant-coefficient quadratic metric gravity. With matter, a
specified source and its own equations must instead be demonstrated; defining
an effective tensor from the metric is insufficient. No relation between
\(r_c\) and \(\mu\) is derived here. This negative dynamical test does not
alter the algebraic horizon calculation for the representative model.

\section{Photon Sphere Structure and Observable Deviation}

A central consequence of the TIG framework is the modification of null geodesic
structure near compact objects. In particular, the location of the photon sphere
provides a direct probe of the underlying gravitational geometry.

The photon sphere is determined by the standard condition for unstable circular
null geodesics:
\begin{equation}
r F'(r) - 2F(r) = 0.
\end{equation}

Within the representative TIG geometry, the metric function takes the form
\begin{equation}
F(r) = 1 - \frac{2M r^2}{r^3 + r_c^3},
\end{equation}
which introduces a characteristic scale \(r_c\) governing deviations from the
classical Schwarzschild geometry.

In the limit \(r_c \to 0\), the model smoothly reduces to the Schwarzschild
solution with
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{ph}}^{\mathrm{GR}} = 3M,
\end{equation}
ensuring consistency with established gravitational theory.

We define the relative deviation from the Schwarzschild photon sphere as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{ph}} =
\frac{r_{\mathrm{ph}}^{\mathrm{TIG}} - 3M}{3M}.
\end{equation}

Numerical solutions of the representative model indicate a systematic inward
shift of the photon sphere for finite core scale. The effect remains negligible
for small \(r_c\), but grows nonlinearly as the characteristic scale approaches
the near-critical regime.

This shift implies a possible contraction of the photon sphere and hence a
correction to the apparent shadow scale relative to General Relativity. At the
present stage this should be interpreted as a model-dependent structural
signature rather than a completed observational forecast.

\section{Observable Implications}

The TIG transition may affect observables sensitive to horizon-scale geometry.
Possible implications include:
\begin{itemize}
\item modifications of black-hole shadow radius;
\item altered photon-sphere structure;
\item changes in near-horizon lensing behavior;
\item modified ringdown or echo-like structures near criticality.
\end{itemize}

At the present stage, these implications are model-dependent research targets.
A prediction requires specified sources, propagation and perturbation equations,
and a demonstrated map from the geometry to the observable. The horizon-root
fold alone does not establish enhanced sensitivity or a critical exponent for
every proposed observable.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

This paper develops the algebraic horizon structure of TIG. Several important
questions remain open.

First, the complete dynamical field equations associated with the TIG
consistency principle must be formulated. Second, stability of the resulting
compact configurations must be analyzed. Third, observational predictions must
be computed within a fully specified metric model.

The present work is an algebraic analysis of a specified representative
geometry with a local fold transition. It is not a complete gravitational
theory, a vacuum solution of the tested quadratic metric action, or a proof of
its own observational predictions.

\section{Conclusion}

For the specified regular mass profile, we derived the exact horizon equation
\begin{equation}
x^3-x^2+\beta^3=0,
\end{equation}
with critical point \(x_c=2/3\), \(\beta_c=(4/27)^{1/3}\). Two positive roots
coalesce in a local nondegenerate fold and disappear above the critical value;
their displacements scale as \((\beta_c-\beta)^{1/2}\).

The cubic degree and the correction parameterization follow from the selected
mass profile, not from a universal cubic requirement for folds. The geometry
has a Schwarzschild limit and a finite-curvature core, but fails the vacuum
trace equation of the tested constant-coefficient quadratic metric action.
A dynamical completion with specified sources, perturbative stability, and
observable predictions must be established separately.
